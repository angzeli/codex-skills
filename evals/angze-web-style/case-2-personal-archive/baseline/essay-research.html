What a decade of moss taught me about attention

The smallest plot in my research is the size of a postcard. Returning to it each spring has changed how I understand evidence, patience, and loss.

2 March 2026 · 11 min read

Plot 17 lives behind a library air vent, where two concrete walls meet at an angle that traps rain. It is not scenic. There are no rare species there, no dramatic colors, no view beyond a bicycle rack. The entire moss patch fits beneath my open hand.

I first measured it in April 2016 as part of a study on moisture refuges in dense neighborhoods. The project ended after eighteen months. Plot 17 did not. Each spring I returned with the same transparent grid, a ruler, a humidity sensor, and a pencil. What began as leftover curiosity became the longest observation in my archive.

The discipline of the same question

The protocol is deliberately plain. I place a ten-centimeter grid over the patch, estimate green cover in each square, record surface temperature and humidity, and make one photograph from a marked point. If the vent is running, I note it. If rain has fallen within twenty-four hours, I note that too. The visit takes twenty minutes.

Repetition does not eliminate judgment. It makes judgment visible. In the first years, my estimates clustered suspiciously around multiples of five. Later, after comparing photographs with my notes, I realized that I consistently underestimated thin, pale growth. The moss had not become easier to see; I had learned where my seeing failed.

Scientific instruments are often described as extensions of the senses. A long protocol works in the opposite direction as well. It trains the observer to become a more honest instrument—one that records its own drift.

When continuity breaks

In 2020 I could not visit the site during the normal April window. A librarian took three photographs using instructions sent by email. The images were generous and useful, but they were not equivalent to the established method: the angle differed, the grid bowed, and no humidity reading was available.

My first impulse was to repair the gap. I considered estimating cover from the photographs and interpolating moisture from a nearby weather station. Instead, the year remains an incomplete row. The blank cells say something true about how knowledge was made under interrupted conditions.

Continuity is not the absence of gaps. It is the practice of keeping gaps legible.

This distinction has guided larger projects since. A dataset becomes trustworthy not when every field is full, but when the route from observation to record can be followed. A clean table can conceal a messy process. I would rather keep the process.

Changes larger than the plot

The moss expanded during the wet summers of 2017 and 2018, retreated after masonry repairs in 2019, and recovered along a new crack two years later. In 2023 the library replaced its ventilation system. Exhaust temperatures fell by nearly four degrees on comparable spring mornings. The patch spread toward the vent for the first time.

These events tempt a neat story: cooler infrastructure permitted recovery. It is plausible, but the record is too small and confounded to carry that conclusion. Shade changed as a nearby tree grew. Cleaning schedules shifted. The particular mosses were never identified to species. The responsible sentence is quieter: after the vent replacement, the association between distance and cover changed.

Restraint can feel unsatisfying, especially when public conversation rewards findings with sharp edges. Yet uncertainty is not fog laid over an otherwise perfect picture. It is part of the picture. Describing its shape is one of the most exact things a researcher can do.

Attention without ownership

Over ten years, Plot 17 acquired importance because I returned to it. The attention was real; the importance was constructed. Countless other patches disappeared without a numbered grid or a folder of photographs. This is a humbling feature of research: we choose a boundary, then risk confusing what falls inside it with what matters.

To counter that tendency, I now end each field visit with five unstructured minutes. I look beyond the plot and write one observation the protocol did not request. The notes include a snail shell, heat from the brick, chewing gum pressed into a crack, a child asking whether moss has roots, and the library’s first open window of spring.

None belongs in the cover analysis. Together they preserve the setting that made the numbers possible. They also remind me that careful attention need not become possession. A place can exceed the purpose we assign it.

The next spring

This year the original transparency finally split at one corner. I made a replacement from the archived dimensions and photographed both grids together before retiring the old one. It was a small act of provenance, and unexpectedly tender.

I do not know how long the study will continue. The library may renovate the wall; I may move too far away; the moss may vanish. Longevity alone does not make a project meaningful. What matters is that the method still asks a live question.

For now, each April, I kneel beside the bicycle rack and place a grid over a patch the size of a postcard. I count what is there. I record what I cannot count. Then I leave the moss to the rest of its year.
